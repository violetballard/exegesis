# Toward Liberatory Leadership Preparation: Redesigning a Professional Practice Doctorate

## Abstract

## Introduction

This paper reports on a long‑running experiment in leadership preparation at a public CSU. Our Educational Leadership EdD is a professional‑practice doctorate designed for working educators and leaders who stay in their roles while earning the degree. It is cohort‑based and organized as a full‑time, three‑year pathway; coursework and dissertation work are framed around real‑world dilemmas that arise in schools, districts, colleges, and community‑based educational settings. Rather than pulling practitioners out of practice, the program aims to make doctoral learning immediately usable in their ongoing work.

Written nearly twenty years ago, our original mission, vision, and program learning outcomes did more than describe the degree; they articulated what counted as legitimate leadership and improvement, shaping routines and assumptions that have accumulated over time. Now entering the program’s second decade, we are redesigning with an explicit twenty‑year horizon. This longer view shifts our focus from short‑term tinkering to durable commitments and structures capable of withstanding governance and resource constraints and the political volatility surrounding equity work in education.

The student body is majority BIPOC and majority women, many bringing anti‑oppressive leadership commitments rooted in lived experience and community accountability. These realities shape our redesign challenge. Cohorts can foster solidarity, shared language, and a learning community that engages honestly with power and institutional harm. At the same time, the intensity of full‑time doctoral study combined with leadership duties, family responsibilities, and community obligations can be emotionally, cognitively, and physically taxing. These pressures are central, not peripheral, to what kinds of learning are realistically possible. They also shape how anti‑oppressive leadership can be practiced within constrained systems under today’s political climate.

### Longitudinal Program Context

Because we are redesigning a long‑running program, we begin by grounding the story in longitudinal data rather than relying solely on intentions and narratives. Cohort enrollment patterns and completion outcomes matter for two reasons. First, they show the baseline conditions under which leadership development occurs. They also look at whether the program can structurally survive for working professionals and whether its design reliably guides students to completion. Second, they provide essential context for interpreting redesign choices. A program that is unstable, shrinking, or losing students is being redesigned under crisis logic. In contrast, a program with steady enrollment and strong completion rates is redesigning from a position of stability: not to “fix failure,” but to align formal commitments with what it can sustainably support. This ensures that the next twenty years will not simply inherit existing logics just because the program meets traditional institutional metrics.

In our case, we have seen steady enrollment over time and consistently high completion rates. We do not treat those outcomes as proof that anti-oppressive leadership development is happening, and we do not reduce program success to institutional metrics. However, these trends matter because they indicate durability. They also sharpen the analytic question: if a program can be structurally stable while still carrying an older neoliberal accountability framing in its official texts, what becomes possible when the formal contract shifts toward abolitionist and humanizing commitments? In other words, the data establishes that the program has been able to persist, and the redesign asks what kind of program it will choose to be.

## Positionality

This work is written from the inside. Our research team includes faculty and program leaders who have designed, taught, advised, and governed this EdD program while also studying it. That proximity can create risk by normalizing blind spots, overlooking harms, or defending decisions we helped make. However, we do not claim neutrality. We treat positionality and power as analytic content, not bias to be erased.

In our team conversations and memos, we returned repeatedly to a practical question: as practitioner-scholars embedded in the program, whose positionality matters for interpretation and redesign? Alumni accounts carry authority because they reveal what the program felt like from the learner’s side, what was survivable, what was damaging, and what graduates could sustain in real institutional settings. Faculty and leadership perspectives carry a different kind of authority because they make visible the design constraints, tradeoffs, and governance dynamics that students cannot always see. Rather than treating one vantage point as objective, we treated tensions across vantage points as data. We look for signals of how legitimacy is produced, how dissonance emerges between equity commitments and institutional realities, and where the program reproduces the very logics it claims to disrupt.

Methodologically, we used structured team debriefs and analytic memos to surface assumptions and to document where we might be protecting a preferred story rather than following evidence. Disagreement and discomfort were treated as prompts to return to the data and strengthen claims. This stance mattered because our work is inherently political. We are redefining leadership preparation, rewriting foundational program documents, and changing program routines. This involves choices about whose expertise counts and what kinds of leadership the institution is willing to recognize.

## Shifting Direction

By the time a program reaches its second decade, it starts to accumulate two things at once: stability and drift. The stability shows up in steady enrollment, very high completion rates, and very high satisfaction levels in yearly surveys. The drift shows up more quietly, in language and routines that keep reproducing an earlier era’s common sense about what leadership is for, what counts as improvement, and what the institution will recognize as rigorous. Over time, that drift can become invisible because nothing is broken in the way institutions usually recognize a crisis. Our yearly survey is an example of this, if we continue to use it as a metric as it has been used in the past, there is a high probability that we will miss issues due to drift.

That tension is the backdrop for our redesign, and it’s also why this story begins with governance.

As we began to plan for the program’s next twenty years, we realized that the most consequential redesign choice was not a course tweak or a single initiative. It was whether we would treat the program’s Mission, Vision, and Program Learning Outcomes as living governance instruments that actually direct decisions, or as legacy text that sits above practice. The original Mission, Vision, and PLOs were written at the foundation of the program nearly twenty years ago. They had become the inherited public contract: the official language that tells students, faculty, and the institution what the program is for and what it produces. If we were serious about aligning the program with anti-oppressive leadership preparation, the first task was to revisit that contract.

In our program, that kind of decision sits with the executive committee, the program’s governing board. The executive committee includes stakeholders from within and across the university and from outside it, bringing together institutional authority, institutional memory, and community-facing accountability. In the early phase of the redesign, the committee unanimously approved a revised Mission and Vision and a revised set of learning outcomes to set the program’s strategic direction for the next twenty years.

That approval did not complete the redesign. It created the conditions for implementation. It stabilized the direction before the work moved into broader engagement and practical translation across stakeholder groups. We are now in the midst of that implementation work: interpreting the commitments with students, alumni, faculty, and partners, and translating them into program structures under real constraints. This is what we mean by consensus-based radicalism: a governance-backed commitment that holds steady enough to be implemented, strengthened through stakeholder engagement rather than diluted by it.

## From Accountability to Abolition

What follows is a focused discourse comparison of the original and revised Mission and Vision statements and a selected set of learning outcomes. The point is not to catalog every change, but to show how the revised documents redefine what counts as legitimate leadership work and what the program is now accountable to.

### Legitimacy

In the original Mission and Vision, legitimacy is anchored in the dominant institutional register of the time. The program frames its purpose as advancing “development, learning, and academic achievement for all students” and “equaliz\[ing\] student access to the cultural capital that a rigorous education based on open-minded inquiry and reflection can provide.” In this frame, equity is narrated through access to institutionally valued goods and outcomes. The implied horizon is reform that remains legible to existing measures of rigor, achievement, and performance.

The revised Vision and Mission set a different horizon of legitimacy. The Vision commits to “Preparing transformative educational leaders who abolish, challenge, and subvert unjust educational spaces while co-creating liberatory and humanizing systems for California public education.” The Mission prepares leaders to “expose and resist inequity” and to “design, nurture, and sustain systems that center joy, healing, belonging, and justice.” Here, legitimacy is not earned through alignment with existing institutional logics; it is earned through the capacity to confront and remake unjust systems while building humanizing conditions as an explicit leadership responsibility.

### Knowledge

The original documents use a broadly recognizable academic register, emphasizing rigor and inquiry, but without naming the interpretive commitments that would shape what counts as rigorous or what kinds of knowledge are necessary to see institutional harm. That ambiguity allows “rigor” to remain compatible with conventional, often depoliticized, epistemologies.

The revised documents make epistemic commitments explicit. They state, “This program operates with full awareness that public education and higher education are shaped by forces that include settler colonialism, white supremacy, capitalism, ableism, and heteropatriarchy.” They also commit to expanding what counts as knowledge and scholarship: “We value lived knowledge, collective accountability, and liberatory practices challenging dominant structures and we frame our program with the goal of expanding what counts as leadership, scholarship, and learning.” The pedagogical stance is likewise explicit: “We reject the banking model of education and seek to bring critical theory informed pedagogy to students across multiple domains.” In other words, the revised text does not just promise rigor; it names the theoretical and epistemic grounds on which rigor will be defined.

### Power

The original learning outcomes include language that presumes leaders operate within existing systems of governance and accountability, for example “Apply knowledge on issues of accountability,” and more managerial outcomes such as “Make complex decisions” and “Apply knowledge of education policy, public financing and budgeting.” Inquiry is framed as institutional application: “Interpret program evaluations, research reports, to identify programmatic applications of research findings,” and “Design and carry out educational research that addresses a practical problem of educational leadership.” Power is present, but mostly as governance fluency and technical competence.

The revised outcomes bring power to the foreground as a practical necessity for justice work. PLO 1 states that students use “the legitimacy of the doctorate and their professional roles to convene stakeholders, influence agendas, and exercise authority in decision-making arenas.” This is a direct claim that leadership development must include access, agenda-setting, and decision-making power, not just values or skills. The revised outcomes also specify interpretive tools for power analysis. PLO 3 states that students’ foundation in “critical theories (e.g., decolonial, critical race, DisCrit, feminist, queer)” equips them to interpret dilemmas of educational practice. That specificity matters because it defines the analytical equipment graduates are expected to use when power is contested and institutional harm is normalized.

### Durability

The original documents presume improvement through institutional processes and do not name resistance as a predictable condition of justice work. Durability is implied through competence and professionalism rather than addressed as a design problem.

The revised outcomes make durability explicit. PLO 6 names “navigational resilience,” anticipating “political resistance, bureaucratic inertia, and gatekeeping” while sustaining equity-centered goals over time. The revised Mission also commits to leaders who “critique and dismantle administrative violence while simultaneously dreaming, designing, and implementing educational ecosystems rooted in collective care and radical possibility.” Taken together, durability is reframed as the capacity to sustain justice commitments under predictable institutional resistance without collapsing into either burnout or compliance.

### From Commitments to Design

The shift in the program documents is the starting condition for the rest of the paper. Once the program formally claims abolitionist, humanizing, and epistemically explicit commitments, the immediate question becomes how those commitments are made actionable without being domesticated by the routines and constraints of a public university. That translation work cannot be treated as a matter of implementation fidelity to statements alone. It requires a theory of what the EdD is, a theory of what leadership for justice demands, a theory of how adults develop the identities and capacities required to sustain that work, and a theory of how institutional change can be pursued inside systems that predictably resist it.

The next section lays out those theoretical foundations as an integrated framework. It explains why we treat the EdD as a professional practice doctorate, why transformative and decolonial leadership provide the moral orientation for redesign, why transformative learning and identity formation are central mechanisms rather than secondary outcomes, and why radical pragmatism is necessary for making justice commitments durable inside constrained institutional contexts.

## Theoretical Foundations for Program Redesign

Preparing leaders capable of advancing collective liberation requires clarity about three foundational questions: What kind of doctoral program are we? What kind of leadership are we preparing? And how must such leadership be cultivated within institutional constraints? Our redesign is grounded in four interdependent domains that answer these questions sequentially:

1. The EdD as a professional practice doctorate for educational leaders;
2. Transformative, decolonizing leadership as moral grounding for the kind of educational leaders we are preparing;
3. Transformative learning and identity formation as the developmental mechanism required to sustain such leadership; and
4. Radical pragmatism as the institutional stance guiding redesign.

Rather than functioning as parallel literatures, these domains form a funnel that moves from epistemological foundation to moral orientation, to developmental necessity, to disciplined institutional action.

### The EdD as a Professional Practice Doctorate

Our program is fundamentally a practitioner-scholar EdD program. It is not a PhD designed to prepare disciplinary researchers for academic knowledge production detached from practice, nor is it an educational leadership credential program focused narrowly on managerial competencies or compliance. Instead, it is a professional practice doctorate grounded in practitioner inquiry and systemic change. The Carnegie Project on the Education Doctorate (CPED) articulates the EdD as preparing educators for the application of appropriate and specific practices, the generation of new knowledge, and stewardship of the profession (CPED, n.d.).

Perry (2016) and Perry et al. (2020) conceptualize the scholarly practitioner as a leader who integrates professional wisdom, rigorous inquiry, and systemic transformation. In this model, knowledge is generated within practice rather than abstracted from it. Inquiry becomes both a methodological stance and a leadership strategy. Flood (2024) similarly argues that centering the scholarly practitioner requires faculty to reorient research coursework toward practitioner positionality and applied knowledge production. Rodríguez López (2024) further differentiates professional wisdom from bias, emphasizing reflexivity, positionality statements, and improvement science safeguards as mechanisms for ensuring rigor in practice-based inquiry. Together, this body of scholarship rejects the notion that practitioner knowledge is inherently less rigorous than traditional academic knowledge.

This epistemological stance grounds our redesign. Leadership preparation begins with the assumption that practitioner inquiry is central to institutional transformation. Knowledge is not neutral; it is embedded within power-laden institutional contexts (Reyes & Zhang, 2024). Practitioner scholarship, therefore, must be accountable to communities most impacted by educational systems. Yet professional practice alone does not determine its moral direction. Without an explicit justice orientation, practitioner inquiry can replicate dominant logics of efficiency, productivity, and deficit framing.

### Transformative, Decolonizing, and Activist Leadership

If we are preparing practitioner-scholars, what kind of leadership are they enacting?

Transformative leadership provides the moral orientation for this work. Shields (2010) defines transformative leadership as beginning with justice and democracy, critiquing inequitable practices, and linking educational leadership to broader social change. Unlike transformational leadership models that prioritize organizational effectiveness, transformative leadership centers moral courage and systemic critique. Currin et al. (2023) extend this orientation through an EdD-activism framework that positions Dissertations in Practice as vehicles for structural change rather than incremental adjustment. Sanders (2024) documents how doctoral study cultivates an explicit equity stance and critical consciousness, while Shaw, Paré-Blagoev, and Quaynor (2024) situate EdD redesign within escalating political backlash against public education.

Decolonial theory deepens this normative orientation. Quijano’s (2000) concept of coloniality of power reveals how racialized and Eurocentric epistemologies structure institutions long after formal colonial rule ends. Maldonado-Torres (2007) extends this analysis through coloniality of being, interrogating whose humanity is recognized within institutional arrangements. Mignolo (2011) calls for epistemic disobedience that delinks from dominant Western knowledge systems to recover pluriversal ways of knowing. These frameworks challenge deficit constructions of students and communities and interrogate normative definitions of professionalism and expertise.

Culturally responsive school leadership (Khalifa, 2018; Khalifa et al., 2016\) further underscores that leadership preparation must center community cultural wealth (Yosso, 2005\) and collective accountability. In our program, this orientation is not peripheral; it is explicitly named in our mission and curricular goals. However, adopting transformative and decolonial commitments does not automatically translate into sustained practice. Leaders must enact these commitments within institutions that are simultaneously sites of oppression and possibility. This tension leads to the developmental question.

### Transformative Learning and Identity Formation

Leadership development is not solely cognitive or technical; it is ontological. Transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000\) suggests that adult learners reconstruct frames of reference through critical reflection and disorienting dilemmas. Marotta (2024) demonstrates how doctoral students experience perspective shifts during EdD programs, often reframing their understanding of equity, leadership, and professional identity. Burriss and Mayers (2024) similarly illustrate how engagement with critical theory reshapes practitioner epistemologies in a practitioner-based EdD context.

Our alumni accounts mirror these findings. Graduates described moments of rupture that recognize internalized deficit logics, confront institutional complicity, and renegotiate their relationship to authority. Drawing on intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1991), borderlands theory (Anzaldúa, 2002), and DisCrit (Annamma et al., 2018; Leonardo & Broderick, 2011), we understand leadership identity as relational, contested, and shaped by systems of oppression. Leaders positioned at intersections of race, gender, sexuality, immigration status, and neurodiversity navigate unique tensions when enacting equity work within institutions that may question their legitimacy.

Transformative learning therefore functions as a developmental mechanism linking critical theory to leadership praxis. Identity alignment is not incidental; it is essential for sustaining justice-centered leadership over time. Cohort-based learning communities (Wenger, 1998; Maher, 2005\) provide relational contexts where such identity work can unfold. However, identity formation alone does not ensure institutional change. Leaders must translate internal transformation into strategic action.

### Radical Pragmatism as Justice-Centered Design

Radical pragmatism provides the integrative bridge between critique and institutional redesign. Mundorf et al. (2019) conceptualize socially just teacher leaders as “radical pragmatists” who wear both a radical hat, imagining a more just future, and a pragmatic hat, navigating present socio-political constraints. Drawing on Postman and Weingartner’s (1970) notion of “soft revolutionaries,” they argue that leaders can work within formal and informal systems to enact incremental but structural change. Valentine (2024) further suggests that radical problems require radical action, yet such action must be grounded in disciplined experimentation and empirical responsiveness.

This orientation resonates with educational design research (McKenney & Reeves, 2018\) and improvement science, both of which emphasize iterative cycles of design, implementation, reflection, and revision. Radical pragmatism rejects both abstract idealism detached from institutional realities and technocratic managerialism divorced from justice. Instead, it positions practitioner inquiry as moral experimentation and program redesign as justice-centered iteration.

Within our redesign, radical pragmatism functions as connective tissue across the framework. The EdD as professional practice provides epistemological grounding (Perry, 2016). Transformative and decolonial leadership provide moral direction (Shields, 2010; Quijano, 2000). Transformative learning clarifies developmental necessity (Mezirow, 2000; Marotta, 2024). Radical pragmatism integrates these strands into disciplined institutional action. We also recognize our own positionality as faculty and designers; rather than striving for false neutrality, we treat positionality as an asset in design work, consistent with practitioner-scholar epistemology and decolonial commitments.

Through this funnel of epistemology, moral orientation, developmental mechanism, and institutional stance our redesign seeks to cultivate practitioner-scholars capable of disciplined experimentation toward collective liberation within systems shaped by coloniality and structural inequity.

## Methodology

This study employs an Educational Design Research (EDR) approach to redesign an EdD program inside a public CSU while generating usable knowledge about justice-centered doctoral preparation. Methodologically, EDR fits our stance of radical pragmatism: we treat program redesign as disciplined experimentation—iterative, documented, and accountable to the constraints and possibilities of institutional life—rather than as a one-time reform.

### Educational Design Research Process

We organized this Educational Design Research around iterative cycles of problem analysis, design production, design evaluation, and reflection/institutionalization (McKenney & Reeves, 2021; Akker et al., 2006). We did not treat these as neutral technical steps. In our context, each phase is explicitly theory-led: practitioner inquiry shapes what counts as a “problem” in a professional practice doctorate (CPED, n.d.; Perry, 2016); transformative and decolonizing leadership commitments shape how design is conducted and whose knowledge is treated as expertise (Shields, 2010; Quijano, 2000); transformative learning and identity formation shape what we treat as evidence of impact (Mezirow, 2000); and radical pragmatism shapes how we engage institutional constraints—not as excuses, but as design conditions that determine whether justice-centered work can endure (Mundorf et al., 2019). This stance also differentiates our work from implementation science approaches that prioritize rapid-cycle improvement without necessarily engaging root causes or identity-level development. We learned from structured evaluation practices (Tran et al., 2024), while retaining EDR’s emphasis on theory-building, contextual explanation, and documented design rationale.

We began with problem analysis as justice-oriented practitioner inquiry. Because practitioner inquiry is a leadership strategy in a practice doctorate, our “problem” was not framed as student deficits, individual shortcomings, or curricular inefficiency. Instead, we interrogated how program structures and norms reproduce inequity and colonial logics, and where alumni experienced dissonance between equity commitments and institutional realities. We drew across multiple sources—annual surveys, alumni interviews, program documents, and institutional data—to identify tensions that were persistent, structural, and consequential for leadership development.

We moved into design production through iterative co-design with students, faculty, alumni, and community partners. We treated design as moral and political work rather than instructional engineering: expanding whose expertise mattered, naming tradeoffs explicitly, and building artifacts capable of carrying equity commitments into everyday routines. Co-design sessions produced working documents and drafts of outcomes and assignments, alongside decision records and analytic memos that captured tensions, turning points, and the rationale behind choices.

We approached design evaluation as evidence of both developmental and institutional change. Rather than asking only whether redesigned structures “worked,” we examined whether they supported shifts in professional identity, critical consciousness, and sustained equity-oriented practice—the developmental outcomes implied by transformative learning and identity formation. At the same time, we examined organizational shifts in curricular routines, governance practices, and program culture. We treated misalignment, resistance, and unintended consequences as design evidence rather than noise, using them to refine both the intervention and the theory claims emerging from the work.

Finally, we treated reflection and institutionalization as a core research object rather than an endpoint. Anchored in radical pragmatism, we analyzed how redesigned practices became routinized, adapted, diluted, or resisted inside existing governance, workload structures, and accountability systems. We repurposed existing bodies (executive committee, curriculum committee, advisory board, and plenary faculty meetings) into structured design-and-learning routines with explicit deliverables, decision records, and follow-up cycles. In this framing, institutionalization includes both the creation of durable structures and the ongoing reflective work required to keep justice commitments from being reduced to compliance under institutional pressure.

### Methods and Data Collection

This study follows a multiple-methods design to trace how redesign processes and redesign outcomes shape one another over time. Our primary evidence comes from in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 34 alumni, intentionally sampled to represent each of the first 14 graduated cohorts. All interviews were conducted under an IRB-approved exempt protocol.

To situate these alumni accounts within the program’s longer trajectory, we also interviewed founding leaders and faculty and examined contextual sources, including program documents, annual survey results, alumni dissertations, and institutional retention and completion data. These materials do not replace alumni perspectives; they help interpret them within evolving program conditions—what was being attempted, what was changing, and what constraints were active at different moments.

Consistent with our theoretical framing, we approached interviews not as “feedback” about program satisfaction but as narrative accounts of practice, identity, and meaning-making. This orientation allowed alumni stories to function as design evidence—revealing how program structures and norms supported, shaped, or constrained justice-centered leadership development in real institutional contexts.

Data Analysis: Reflexive Thematic Analysis, Narrative Sense-Making, and LLM-Supported Memoing

We analyzed interview and document data using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022\) supported by narrative sense-making practices (treating accounts as situated stories with context, turning points, and identity claims). Analysis proceeded iteratively: researchers read transcripts in full, generated initial observations, and wrote analytic memos to capture emerging interpretations, questions, tensions, and positionality-reflections. Memos were treated as a core analytic product used to guide team debriefs, surface assumptions, and document how interpretations changed across cycles of redesign.

A distinctive feature of our analytic workflow was the use of locally hosted large language models (LLMs) as confidential research assistants to support organization, retrieval, and dialogic reflection without sending sensitive data to third-party servers. The LLMs were used to support (not substitute for) interpretation. Practically, this meant: humans retained analytic authority; models were used to generate alternative framings, surface possible patterns, and draft provisional summaries that were checked against transcripts; and all meaningful interpretive decisions were made by the research team.

To keep LLM use grounded, we worked from a structured document hierarchy in which transcript excerpts fed summaries, and summaries fed memos (with memos representing the highest level of synthesis). Where the LLM generated suggestions, we treated them as prompts for closer reading: convergences were used to strengthen warrants, and divergences were used to locate disconfirming evidence and refine themes. For transparency and rigor, we maintained an audit trail that included memo versions, meeting notes, and (when used) LLM prompts/outputs tied back to underlying source materials.

Across the process, we applied three guardrails aligned with our justice-centered theoretical framework: positionality and power were treated as analytic content, not bias to be erased; identity development and shifts in leadership practice were treated as central outcomes rather than soft byproducts; and institutional constraints were analyzed as part of the design problem—keeping recommendations feasible without surrendering the redesign’s radical intent.
